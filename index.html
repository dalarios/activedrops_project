One cell. A complete mechanical behavior.

How does a cell program its mechanics?

Lacrymaria olor can extend a slender neck many body lengths, sweep through its surroundings, and retract within seconds. A single cell produces this coordinated behavior by reorganizing its internal architecture.

Its motion makes the underlying question visible: how do genetically encoded, force-generating molecules give rise to organized behavior in space and time?

The biological premise

Cellular behavior begins with motors and filaments.

Molecular motors convert nucleotide hydrolysis into force and translocation along cytoskeletal filaments. When many motors and filaments interact, their local forces collectively reorganize the network into motion and structure.

Force generation

Motors move along filament tracks.

This basic mechanochemical operation drives transport, division, remodeling, and motility.

Self-organization

Many local forces become collective dynamics.

Motor–filament systems can align, flow, rotate, bundle, or contract without a central controller.

Cellular regulation

Cells harness and reshape this intrinsic capacity.

Signaling, expression, crosslinkers, boundaries, and other regulators determine where, when, and how it is deployed.

The biological question

How does motor sequence encode the temporal self-organization of a microtubule network—and can reconfiguring the motor's coupled mechanochemical architecture rewrite that behavior?

Eukaryotic genomes encode multiple motor families and often especially diverse kinesin repertoires. Each sequence specifies a coupled mechanochemical architecture; together, those motors define part of the cell's repertoire of mechanical capabilities.

motor sequence→biochemistry→collective dynamics→mechanical behavior

The ActiveDROPS pipeline

From motor-coding DNA to mechanical phenotype.

ActiveDROPS expresses a kinesin–mVenus motor inside the same droplet whose microtubule mechanics are measured. Imaging follows the dynamics, and a mechanics-only hidden semi-Markov model maps each complete program into a shared behavior space.

The experimental logic

From natural diversity to a rewritten phenotype.

Each result creates the question answered by the next: identify mechanical diversity, establish its biochemical basis, then change the motor sequence itself.

01 · Mechanical diversity

Motor sequences generate three mechanical phenotypes.

Across twelve kinesin-related sequences, ActiveDROPS reveals Slow-Sustained, Fast-Burst, and ThTr-specific Multiphase programs. The phenotypes differ in activation, velocity, persistence, state progression, and final organization.

A mechanics-only hidden semi-Markov model compares the complete programs and locates every measured phenotype in a shared behavior space.

02 · Biochemical basis

The phenotype organization persists across scales.

ATP-dependent gliding kinetics and simulated motor–tubulin interactions preserve the Slow-Sustained–Fast-Burst organization first observed in ActiveDROPS. The collective phenotypes therefore have a sequence-dependent biochemical basis.

Individual exceptions matter: no single measured coordinate determines a collective program that integrates expression, stepping, attachment, force generation, and network remodeling.

Watch the associated movie ↗

03 · Central experiment

Coupled motor architecture can rewrite the temporal program.

Recombining coupled regions of Slow-Sustained K401 and Fast-Burst Kif3 creates SFS, a motor that activates rapidly and sustains motion for hours. Its Fast-Sustained mechanical phenotype is absent from both parents.

The exchanged regions coordinate nucleotide processing, microtubule interaction, and force transmission. Their effects depend on sequence context and on the coupled architecture in which they act.

Watch the associated movie ↗

04 · Regulatory extension

Gene dosage changes which phases one motor reaches.

Changing ThTr DNA dosage changes its motor-expression dynamics and redirects the collective program: the network remains nematic, progresses to rotation, or continues through rotation to contraction.

This secondary result shows how regulation can deploy multiple phases accessible to one sequence; it does not define a sharp concentration threshold for active motor.

The central answer

Motor sequence encodes a temporal program through coupled mechanochemical architecture.

Natural diversity

Motor sequences generate reproducibly different programs of microtubule self-organization.

Biochemical basis

The phenotype organization recurs in motor-scale kinetics and modeled molecular interactions.

Causal rewrite

Reconfiguring coupled sequence regions combines parental dynamics into a phenotype unavailable to either parent.

In brief

ActiveDROPS is an experimental–computational pipeline that begins with motor-coding DNA and ends with a mechanical phenotype located in a shared behavior space.

Natural motor sequences generate distinct collective programs; motor-scale analyses establish their biochemical basis; and recombining coupled motor architecture creates a temporal phenotype absent from both parents.

The work provides a foundation for reverse engineering how sequence-encoded, self-organizing cytoskeletal components generate cellular mechanical behavior.
